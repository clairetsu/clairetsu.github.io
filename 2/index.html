Part 1.1: Convolutions from Scratch

Both implementations flip the kernel 180°, zero pad the image by (kernel size − 1) on every side, and return the "full" output. The four-loop version walks over every output pixel and every kernel entry. The two-loop version replaces the kernel loops with a single elementwise multiply and sum over the window. 

def conv_four_loops(img, kernel):
    img = img.astype(np.float64)
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    flipped = np.rot90(kernel, k=2)
    padded = np.pad(img, ((kh - 1, kh - 1), (kw - 1, kw - 1)), mode="constant", constant_values=0)
    out_h = padded.shape[0] - kh + 1
    out_w = padded.shape[1] - kw + 1
    out = np.zeros((out_h, out_w))
    for i in range(out_h):
        for j in range(out_w):
            total = 0.0
            for u in range(kh):
                for v in range(kw):
                    total += flipped[u][v] * padded[i+u][j+v]
            out[i, j] = total
    return out

def conv_two_loops(img, kernel):
    img = img.astype(np.float64)
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    flipped = np.rot90(kernel, k=2)
    padded = np.pad(img, ((kh - 1, kh - 1), (kw - 1, kw - 1)), mode="constant", constant_values=0)
    out_h = padded.shape[0] - kh + 1
    out_w = padded.shape[1] - kw + 1
    out = np.zeros((out_h, out_w))
    for i in range(out_h):
        for j in range(out_w):
            out[i, j] = np.sum(flipped * padded[i:i+kh, j:j+kw])
    return out

Comparison with scipy

Both match scipy.signal.convolve2d(mode="full", boundary="fill") exactly. The four-loop version is by far the slowest, since Python runs every multiply-add itself, so its runtime grows with kernel size (the 9 × 9 box is much slower than Dx and Dy). The two-loop version hands each window's multiply and sum to numpy, so it's much faster on large kernels, though for a 2-entry kernel like Dx the gain is small. Scipy runs entirely in compiled code and is faster than both. For boundaries, zero padding treats everything outside the image as black. That darkens the border of the box-filtered image and creates fake edges along the border for Dx and Dy. Scipy offers the same zero fill, plus wrap and symmetric (mirror) padding, which avoid this.

Part 1.2: Finite Difference Operator

The gradient magnitude is √(dx² + dy²), and the edge image keeps pixels with magnitude above 0.25. Lower thresholds picked up the grass as edges; higher ones started breaking up the man's outline. 0.25 removed almost all of the grass noise while keeping the man, camera, and tripod intact, at the cost of most of the background buildings.

Part 1.3: Derivative of Gaussian (DoG) Filter

I built a 7 × 7 Gaussian (σ = 1) by taking the outer product of cv2.getGaussianKernel with its transpose, blurred the cameraman, and repeated Part 1.2. Compared with the finite differences, the grass noise is mostly gone and the edges are thicker and more continuous. A threshold of 0.08 was enough, which also kept the background buildings.

Convolving the Gaussian with Dx and Dy gives the two DoG filters below, so each direction needs only one convolution on the image. The results match the two-step version, since convolution is associative, apart from tiny differences at the image border where padding happens at a different step.

Part 2.1: Image "Sharpening"

A Gaussian blur keeps only the low frequencies, so subtracting it from the original leaves the high frequencies: edges and fine detail. Adding α times that detail back makes edges stronger, which reads as sharper. Since every step is linear, it collapses into one filter, the unsharp mask: (1 + α) · impulse − α · Gaussian. I used σ = 2. Small α looks naturally crisper; by α = 2, halos appear along strong edges, and α = 5 is clearly overprocessed.

Part 2.2: Hybrid Images

A hybrid adds the high frequencies of one image (image minus its Gaussian blur, σ1) to the low frequencies of another (Gaussian blur, σ2). Up close, the high frequencies dominate; from far away, only the low frequencies remain visible.

Derek + Nutmeg: full process

I aligned the images on the eyes, rotating Nutmeg to match Derek, and cropped away the padding left by alignment. Derek is the high-pass image and Nutmeg the low-pass one.

Cutoff frequency choice

I tuned by viewing the hybrid at full size and shrunk by up to 8×, which simulates stepping back. σ1 = 5 keeps Derek clearly visible up close. Lowering σ2 to 6 kept enough of Nutmeg's features to recognize her from a distance, without her showing through strongly up close.

Part 2.3: Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks

Each Gaussian level blurs the previous one (σ = 2, 8 levels, no subsampling), and each Laplacian level is the difference between consecutive Gaussian levels, with the last Gaussian level as the final one. Laplacian levels are contrast-stretched for display.

Part 2.4: Multiresolution Blending

Each Laplacian level of the apple and orange is blended with the matching level of a Gaussian stack of the mask, then all levels are summed. The mask is a step function, pre-blurred slightly so even the finest level doesn't switch in one pixel. The mask's stack alone sets how wide each transition is, so I gave it a larger σ (15) than the image stacks. With the same σ as the images, the coarse transitions were too narrow and left a visible seam.

Clock in a sunflower (irregular mask)

I aligned the two by clicking the edges of the clock face and the sunflower's center, so both circles are centered and the same size, then used a circular mask.

Cookie + moon

The cookie photo has a light background and the moon a black one, which no seam can hide. I replaced the moon's background with the cookie's before blending, so the vertical seam only has to blend the two objects.

What I Learned

The most important thing I learned was how to think about images in terms of frequencies. It's an overarching idea behind every transformation in this project: sharpening boosts the high frequencies, hybrid images combine the low frequencies of one image with the high frequencies of another, and multiresolution blending gives each frequency band its own transition width.
